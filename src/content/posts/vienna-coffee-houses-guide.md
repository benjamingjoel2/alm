The coffee arrives on a small tray, and next to the cup there is always a glass of water. The table is marble, the chair is bentwood, and somewhere behind you a newspaper rustles on its wooden holder. Nobody hurries you towards the bill. In a Viennese coffee house, that is the whole point.

Vienna has cafés of every kind, from third-wave espresso bars to cake shops, but the traditional coffee house is something particular. It is part living room, part reading room and part institution, and it runs on habits that are easy to pick up once someone explains them. This is our short guide to how it works.

## A tradition with official standing

Viennese coffee house culture is not just a tourist label. Since 2011 it has been listed in Austria's national inventory of intangible cultural heritage, kept by the [Austrian Commission for UNESCO](https://www.unesco.at/en/culture/intangible-cultural-heritage/inventory/element/viennese-coffee-house-culture). The entry describes the typical elements: small marble-topped tables, Thonet chairs, alcoves, newspaper tables and interior details in the style of Historicism.

It also contains the line that best explains the whole thing. The coffee house, the entry says, is a place "in which time and space are consumed, but only the coffee is listed on the bill."

The history is long. The commission's entry traces the story back to the late 17th century, and notes that in 1720 Café Kramer on the Graben became the first coffee house to offer newspapers to its customers. Women were admitted from 1856. The tourist board adds that the coffee house reached its heyday at the end of the 19th century, when people went there [to read, play, argue and write](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/wiener-kaffeehaus-fun-facts-433242).

## The unwritten rules

None of this is written on the menu, but it is how a traditional house works.

- **The glass of water comes with the coffee.** The tourist board explains that it was first served so guests had somewhere to put their spoon, and later became a way for owners to show off the quality of Vienna's mountain spring water. It is still [a seal of quality](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/wiener-kaffeehaus-fun-facts-433242) today.
- **You can stay.** One coffee buys you the table for as long as you want it. The tourist board's [media text on the Viennese coffeehouse](https://b2b.wien.info/resource/blob/321962/610709f95c861f5e8d6850710c788ac3/artikel-db-kaffeehaus-en-en-data.docx) describes the city's coffee houses as the ideal place to take time out, open to all, where people go to read the paper, play billiards or chess and talk to friends and strangers alike.
- **The newspapers are there to be read.** Traditional houses usually keep a wide range of national and international papers for guests. [Café Prückel](https://www.prueckel.at/) lists the most important newspapers and, naturally, a glass of water with your coffee among the things guests can expect; [Café Sperl](https://www.cafesperl.at/) offers the day's papers and a selection of the international press.
- **The waiters run the room.** In the classic houses the staff are still dressed in black, according to the same media text. At Café Korb, wien.info says, [the real bosses are the waiters](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/in-the-old-city-348958), always with a witty remark ready. Catch their eye when you want to order, and again when you want to pay; nobody will hurry you in between.

A good rhythm for a first visit is simple: find a table, order a coffee and perhaps a cake, borrow a newspaper, and let an hour pass.

## How to order

The tourist board's media text puts the first rule plainly: never order just a "coffee". Viennese coffee houses offer [more than 40 kinds](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/wiener-kaffeehaus-fun-facts-433242), and although not every house serves all of them, these are the names you will see most often on the menu, as [the Vienna Tourist Board defines them](https://b2b.wien.info/resource/blob/321962/610709f95c861f5e8d6850710c788ac3/artikel-db-kaffeehaus-en-en-data.docx):

- **Melange:** a shot of espresso lengthened slightly with hot water, topped up with hot milk and milk foam. The tourist board calls it the Viennese counterpart to the cappuccino, and [milder](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/wiener-kaffeehaus-fun-facts-433242). This is the safe first order.
- **Schwarzer or Mokka:** a strong black coffee without milk.
- **Kleiner Brauner and Grosser Brauner:** a small or large black coffee with a shot of cream.
- **Verlängerter:** a small black coffee lengthened with hot water, with or without milk. The tourist board compares it to an Americano.
- **Einspänner:** a large Mokka in a glass with plenty of whipped cream. The name means a carriage drawn by a single horse, and the story goes that the cream kept the driver's coffee warm. Today it comes in a glass mug with a handle.
- **Kapuziner:** a small black coffee with a few drops of cream.
- **Franziskaner:** a light Melange topped with whipped cream.
- **Fiaker:** Mokka in a glass with a shot of rum.

Espresso, cappuccino and caffè latte now appear on many traditional menus too, so you will not be stuck. But a Melange or a Kleiner Brauner is the order that fits the room.

## Something sweet

Coffee and cake belong together here. The tourist board's guide to [Viennese pastry culture](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/viennese-cuisine/pastries-1063158) is a good primer: Sachertorte and Apfelstrudel are the famous names, but the tradition runs much wider, shaped by the kitchens of the old Habsburg lands. Bohemian Buchteln, yeast-dough buns filled with Powidl plum jam, are one example, and Kaiserschmarrn, the torn, sugared pancake, is another.

The coffee houses each have their specialities, and these are the ones the tourist board and the houses themselves point to:

- **Buchteln at Hawelka,** served fresh from the oven [each evening from 8 pm](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/viennese-cuisine/pastries-1063158).
- **The Sperl Torte at Café Sperl,** the house cake, according to wien.info's [top traditional coffee houses](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/top-traditional-coffee-houses-in-vienna-361666).
- **Apple strudel at Café Korb,** which wien.info says you simply must try.
- **Scheiterhaufen and banana slice at Café Diglas** on Wollzeile, both of which wien.info says the café is known for across the city.

Most houses also serve proper food, from soups and goulash to Wiener schnitzel, so a coffee house can easily become lunch.

## A handful of classic houses

There is no single best coffee house, and we are not going to rank them. These six are a good start, described from their own websites and the tourist board's listings. Check opening hours before you go, as they change with the seasons.

**Café Central.** In Palais Ferstel on Herrengasse since 1876, Café Central is known for its Venetian-style courtyard, its in-house patisserie and the literary regulars of a century ago, among them Peter Altenberg and Arthur Schnitzler. It is [closed for a full renovation from 16 March 2026](https://www.cafecentral.wien/en/), with reopening scheduled for winter 2026; in the meantime the team welcomes guests at DECENTRAL on the Freyung.

**Café Landtmann.** Opened on the Ringstrasse in 1873, the year of the World Expo, Landtmann is one of the few remaining Ringstrasse cafés and sits next to the Burgtheater. Its [own site](https://www.landtmann.at/en/) describes specialty coffees, pastries and cakes made fresh daily, and the evening transformation into "Landtmann at Night" with music and Art Deco projections on the ceiling.

**Café Sperl.** Built in 1880 to plans by the Ringstrasse architects Gross and Jelinek and taken over by the Sperl family that same year, [Café Sperl](https://www.cafesperl.at/) has kept its original interior, marble tables, Thonet chairs and billiard tables. It sits between Mariahilfer Strasse and the Naschmarkt, and wien.info lists piano music on Sunday afternoons.

**Café Hawelka.** Josefine and Leopold Hawelka opened the café on Dorotheergasse in 1936, and it [is still run by the Hawelka family](https://www.hawelka.at/). The tourist board describes it as an artists' café and a Viennese institution, and the evening Buchteln are the reason many people come.

**Café Prückel.** On the Stubenring since 1903, the [Prückel](https://www.prueckel.at/) calls itself the Viennese "second living room". Its interior was remodelled in the 1950s by the architect Oswald Haerdtl, and wien.info notes that it still looks as it did then, down to the lamps and umbrella stand.

**Demel.** The former imperial and royal court confectioner on Kohlmarkt has been [tempting locals and travellers since 1786](https://www.demel.at/en/). Wien.info says its window displays still draw people in with sweets Empress Elisabeth once loved, and that its serving staff have been exclusively women for 200 years, the Demlerinnen in their black and white uniforms.

## Practical notes

- **Expect a wait at the famous names.** Wien.info notes that long lines often form outside Café Central, and the side streets of the 1st district have plenty of quieter alternatives.
- **Some houses offer piano music.** Wien.info lists Sunday afternoon piano at Café Sperl and evening piano three nights a week at Café Prückel. Check current days before planning around them.
- **Summer Sundays vary.** Café Sperl, for example, closes on Sundays in July and August, according to wien.info.
- **The Vienna City Card brings discounts at some cafés.** Wien.info lists a one-off discount at Café Landtmann and Café Museum for cardholders.
- **Do not rush.** Ordering a second coffee is welcome; ordering nothing more is fine too.
