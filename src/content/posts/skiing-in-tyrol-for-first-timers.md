Just after nine on a winter morning, the ski bus pulls up at the valley station and empties out: children in bright helmets clumping after their instructors, a few experts already looking up the mountain, and the first-timers carrying their hire skis a little awkwardly. Up top, the snow has been groomed overnight into neat corduroy and the practice area is still quiet.

Tyrol is one of the easiest places in the Alps to learn. There are hundreds of ski schools, plenty of small, gentle ski areas, and many resorts can be reached by train. The hard part is choosing. Here is how we would approach a first ski week, from picking a resort to the lunch you will earn by midday.

## Choosing a resort as a beginner

Big names are not always best for a first week. Tirol's tourist board has a whole [list of small ski areas](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/resorts-for-beginners-returners) suited to beginners and returners, pointing to quieter pistes, shorter lift queues and manageable terrain. Larger resorts can work just as well if they have good practice areas and an easy way back to the village. A few that fit:

- **Seefeld.** The small Gschwandtkopf area is described by Tirol's tourist board as [perfectly geared towards newcomers](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/resorts-with-railway-station), a few minutes by ski bus from Seefeld station. Next door, the Rosshütte has the [Sportis Kinderland](https://www.seefeld.com/en/rosshuette-sportis-kinderland.html), a 20,000 m² practice area kept separate from the main runs, with a uniform gentle gradient and covered magic carpets.
- **Alpbachtal.** The Ski Juwel Alpbachtal Wildschönau combines small valley lifts with higher, more varied pistes, and the JUPPI-DO Kinderland where children learn their first turns, [according to Tirol's tourist board](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/ski-regions-for-families/ski-holiday-in-the-alpbachtal).
- **Kitzbühel.** KitzSki is a big area, but Tirol's tourist board describes it as suitable for [families and beginners as well as experts](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/resorts-with-railway-station), with ski buses linking the valley stations and villages.
- **The Zillertal.** Reached via Jenbach station and the Zillertalbahn or buses, the Mayrhofen lifts offer an [extensive network of pistes](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/resorts-with-railway-station) for beginners, families and experts.

### Innsbruck and the SKI plus CITY Pass

Innsbruck itself is a strong option if you want variety. The [SKI plus CITY Pass](https://www.ski-plus-city.com/en/) covers 12 ski areas around Innsbruck and in the Stubai Valley, from the Stubai Glacier to smaller areas such as the Serlesbahnen above Mieders, plus 23 city sights and attractions. Ski buses in the Stubai Valley and Innsbruck region are included if you have the Welcome Card, which your accommodation issues free for stays of at least two nights. The pass runs for consecutive days. A beginner can learn on a gentle slope in the morning and spend a cold afternoon in a museum. Check current validity and fares on the pass's website.

## Ski school and equipment hire

Book lessons. Tirol has [more than 390 ski schools](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/ski-schools), and the tourist board lists those with its "Quality Award" by region, including the Innsbruck area, the Stubai Valley and Kitzbühel. The [Ski Juwel Alpbachtal Wildschönau](https://www.skijuwel.com/en/winter/ski-schools-and-ski-rental) calls courses a particularly effective way to learn, for adults and children alike, and suggests asking the ski schools directly about each season's programme.

There is no need to buy equipment for a first trip. In the Ski Juwel, for example, hire shops sit in the village centres and at selected mountain stations, and the resort suggests contacting your chosen shop in advance. Whatever you use, Tirol's tourist board [recommends](https://www.tyrol.com/travel-service/how-to-tirol/skiing-rules-of-behaviour-fis-rules) having bindings set for your weight, height and ability by a specialist shop, and boots that fit snugly without pressure points. Tell the shop honestly that you are a beginner.

## Piste colours and the FIS rules

Austrian pistes come in three colours, and Tirol's tourist board sets out the [definitions](https://www.tirol.at/reiseservice/how-to-tirol/verhaltensregeln-skifahren) (in German):

- **Blue:** easy pistes, whose gradient may not exceed 25 per cent except for short stretches in open terrain.
- **Red:** intermediate pistes, with a gradient of no more than 40 per cent except for short stretches.
- **Black:** difficult pistes, steeper than the limits for red.

There is no green. Your first days will be on practice slopes and then blues; the ski school will tell you when you are ready for red.

Everyone on the piste is expected to follow the International Ski and Snowboard Federation's [ten rules of conduct](https://assets.fis-ski.com/f/252177/49280306c8/fis-code-of-conduct-for-skiers-and-snowboarders-en.pdf). In short:

- Ski in control, adapting your speed to your ability, the terrain, snow, weather and crowds.
- The skier coming from behind must choose a line that does not endanger those ahead, and leave enough space when overtaking.
- Look up and down the slope before setting off or joining a run.
- Avoid stopping in narrow places or where visibility is restricted, and move clear quickly after a fall.
- If walking, keep to the side of the piste. Respect all signs and markings.
- Help at accidents, and exchange names and addresses afterwards, witnesses included.

On helmets, Tirol's tourist board is clear: there is [no legal helmet requirement](https://www.tyrol.com/travel-service/how-to-tirol/skiing-rules-of-behaviour-fis-rules) in Tyrol for adults or for children, although some other Austrian states require helmets for under-15s. It strongly recommends them for adults and says children should always wear one.

## Avalanches and staying on the piste

Beginners have no business off-piste, but avalanche awareness still matters. Tirol's tourist board [notes](https://www.tirol.at/reiseservice/how-to-tirol/verhaltensregeln-skifahren) that the avalanche situation must be taken into account even when skiing on the piste, and the FIS rules require you to respect all signs and markings. A closed run or a barrier across a slope is there for a reason.

The [Euregio Avalanche Report](https://avalanche.report/) is published jointly by the avalanche warning services of Tyrol, South Tyrol and Trentino, in English among other languages. Its [handbook](https://avalanche.report/content/education/handbook/en.html) explains that in winter it appears at least once a day, with a forecast issued at 5 p.m. for the following day. Its intended readers include the safety authorities of cable cars and lifts as well as freeriders and ski tourers in unsecured terrain. The handbook also stresses that a regional forecast cannot replace an on-the-spot assessment. Off-piste skiing is a separate discipline, and not one for a first trip.

## When to go and how to get there

Tirol's tourist board says [most resorts open at the beginning of December](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/life-hacks-for-your-skiing-holiday), and the [glacier ski areas](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing) start earlier, as early as September in some cases. The Christmas peak is usually followed by quieter days in January, known locally as the *Jännerloch*, when pistes and lift queues are calmer. Late season brings longer, warmer days, with skiing still possible in April and the glaciers running well into spring. Opening dates depend on snow, so check your resort before booking.

The train makes Tyrol simple. Tirol's [rail page](https://www.tyrol.com/travel-service/arrival/by-train) lists direct Railjets from Vienna to Innsbruck in 4 hours 17 minutes on the fastest service, several daily direct trains from Zurich in about three and a half hours, and eight direct trains a day from Munich. From London, the journey via Paris and Munich takes about 11 hours, and ÖBB's Nightjet runs daily from Hamburg and Amsterdam. Innsbruck Hauptbahnhof is the natural arrival point, and many resorts have their own station nearby. Book on [ÖBB](https://www.oebb.at/en/) and check current fares there. In the resorts, the tourist board notes that [ski buses](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/life-hacks-for-your-skiing-holiday) take you to the lifts and back free of charge.

## Lunch on the mountain and après-ski

The mountain lunch is half the point. Huts and restaurants on the slopes serve hearty Tyrolean food, and the tourist board's [list of dishes to try](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/food/must-eats) is a good menu guide: *Kasspatzln* (cheese noodles), *Tiroler Gröstl* (fried potatoes and meat), dumplings of every kind, and *Kaiserschmarrn*, the torn, sugared pancake it calls the centrepiece of every mountain hut. Lunch is a sit-down affair, so leave time for it.

Après-ski in Tyrol ranges, in the tourist board's [words](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/apres-ski), from traditional to hip-urban, folk to rock. The etiquette is simple:

- **Après means after.** Tirol's tourist board [reminds skiers](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/life-hacks-for-your-skiing-holiday) that the first FIS rule applies: do not endanger others. Avoid alcohol while skiing, since it slows your reactions.
- **Change for town.** In Innsbruck, the tourist board asks people heading to bars and clubs in the city to go in [ordinary clothes](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/apres-ski), not ski gear.
- **Mind the queue.** When queuing for lifts, the same tourist board's [tips](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/life-hacks-for-your-skiing-holiday) ask you to hold skis upright and not to push.

## Practical notes

- Book ski school early for school holidays, and hire equipment in the resort.
- Wear a helmet, goggles and sun cream; the [tourist board recommends](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/skiing/life-hacks-for-your-skiing-holiday) SPF 50 even on cloudy days.
- Warm up before your first run, and take breaks; tiredness raises the risk of accidents.
- Check the [avalanche report](https://avalanche.report/) and stay on open, marked pistes.
- In a mountain emergency, call 140 (Alpine emergency) or 112, as [Tirol's tourist board advises](https://tyrol.com/travel-service/how-to-tirol/hiking-rules-of-behaviour).
- Travel by train to Innsbruck and use resort ski buses rather than driving.

A first ski week is about small wins: the first linked turns, the first blue run from top to bottom, the first long lunch in the sun. Pair your trip with Advent and you can add the [Christmas markets](/stories/austria-christmas-markets/) to the evenings. Come back in summer, when the same lifts carry walkers up to the huts, and read our guide to [hut-to-hut hiking in the Austrian Alps](/stories/hut-to-hut-hiking-austrian-alps/).
