Late afternoon on a hut terrace, the last walkers of the day come up the path in ones and twos, unclip their packs and swap their boots for something softer at the door. Inside, long wooden tables are being laid for dinner. Below, the valley is already in shadow; above, the ridges still hold the sun. This is what a night in an Austrian Alpine Club hut feels like, and stringing several of them together on foot is one of the best ways we know to see the Alps.

It is also simpler than it looks. The huts run on a shared set of rules, the trails are graded and signposted, and booking a bed now mostly happens online. Here is how it works, from the first reservation to the last descent.

## Alm, hut and Alpine Club hut

This blog takes its name from the *Alm*, the high summer pasture. Tirol's tourist board [explains](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/hiking/easy-walking-routes/hut-hikes/what-is-a-mountain-pasture) that the word covers not just the hut but the surrounding land, often many hectares of meadow and forest. As the snow melts, farmers drive cattle, horses, sheep and goats up from the valley to graze through the summer. On some, the *Sennalmen*, the milk is made into cheese and butter on site. You will pass plenty of Almen on the trail, and many serve hearty regional food to walkers.

An Alpine Club hut is something different: a refuge built for walkers and climbers. The UK section of the Austrian Alpine Club [suggests](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/alpine-huts.php) thinking "mountain inn situated above the valley" rather than "garden shed". Most are substantial buildings sleeping anywhere from 20 to 200 people, staffed in season by a hut warden (*Hüttenwirt*) and team. Each Austrian club hut belongs to a local section of the club, which looks after the building.

The Austrian, German and South Tyrolean clubs together run about 550 of these huts in categories I and II, and all of them follow one rulebook, the [Hut and Tariff Regulations (HÜOTO)](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/Huts/Hut-and-Tariff-Regulations-HueOTO-2024-EN.pdf). You do not need to be a member to stay: Tirol's tourist board [confirms](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/hiking/refuge-huts) that non-members are welcome, while members get reduced rates and other benefits.

## When the huts open and how to book

There is no single opening date. The Austrian Alpine Club publishes a [list of opening times](https://www.alpenverein.at/portal/huetten-wege/huettenoeffnungszeiten.php) for its staffed huts each summer, and it shows the pattern clearly: higher huts open around the middle of June and close in September or early October, while lower huts can open in May and stay open into October. In 2026, for example, the Innsbrucker Hütte (2,369 m) on the Stubai High Trail was listed from 19 June to early October, and the Badener Hütte (2,608 m) from 3 July to 12 September. The club warns that dates can shift at short notice with the weather, so check with the hut before you go.

To find and book a hut:

- Use the club's [Hut Finder](https://www.alpenverein.at/huetten/), which covers more than 560 huts in Austria, Germany and South Tyrol and links to [Hut Reservation](https://www.hut-reservation.org/), the online booking system.
- Hut Reservation can be switched to English, and some bookings need a debit or credit card to confirm, according to the [club's UK section](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/alpine-huts.php). Huts outside the system are booked directly with the hut.
- Wardens may take advance bookings for up to 90 per cent of the beds, may ask for a deposit and may charge for cancellations or no-shows, [under the HÜOTO](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/Huts/Hut-and-Tariff-Regulations-HueOTO-2024-EN.pdf). Book early for July and August weekends.

### Should you join the Alpine Club?

If you plan more than a night or two, it is worth considering. Members pay reduced overnight rates in club huts, and every hut must offer at least one *Bergsteigeressen* (mountaineers' meal, with at least one vegetarian option) to members at a discount. Membership also includes the [Alpenverein Weltweit Service](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/AWS-Insurance.php), insurance cover for rescue after accidents during leisure activities, plus repatriation and medical treatment abroad; read the policy terms before relying on it. Through a reciprocal rights agreement, the same benefits apply in huts of many other European clubs. Residents outside the UK can join any section through alpenverein.at. The UK section, which takes members from anywhere, [notes](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/contact-us/frequently-asked-questions.php) that membership is active as soon as you apply and pay, with a digital card straight away.

## Hut etiquette

Hut life runs on a few simple rules, most of them set out in the [HÜOTO](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/Huts/Hut-and-Tariff-Regulations-HueOTO-2024-EN.pdf):

- **Sign the hut book** on arrival. It is recommended that you note your planned route and mobile number, which helps rescuers if something goes wrong.
- **Boots stay downstairs.** Mountain boots may not be worn in sleeping areas. Some huts have communal hut shoes, but the club's UK section [suggests](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/alpine-huts.php) carrying light slippers of your own.
- **Use a hut sleeping bag.** A sheet sleeping bag (a liner) is compulsory in every sleeping place. Huts provide pillows and blankets; the liner protects the bedding and cuts down on laundry in the mountains.
- **Respect Hüttenruhe.** Quiet hours normally run from 22:00 to 06:00, and the actual time is posted in the hut. Early starters pack the night before and leave quietly.
- **Eat what the hut cooks.** Bringing your own food is not allowed, except for members in a designated self-catering area. At least one hot meal is offered between 12:00 and 20:00.
- **Take your rubbish home.** Smoking is forbidden inside, and music players are not allowed except with headphones for weather and avalanche forecasts.

Most beds are in shared rooms: either *Mehrbettzimmer*, rooms with bunk beds, or the traditional *Matratzenlager*, where sleeping places lie side by side on a wide platform. Lights are often switched off at night, and water can be scarce at higher huts, so wash sparingly. Tirol's tourist board also lists [cash](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/hiking/refuge-huts) among the essentials to bring, and warns that Wi-Fi is rare and mobile reception often limited.

## What to pack

Keep the pack light; you carry everything, every day. Drawing on the [club's hut advice](https://www.alpenverein.at/britannia/membership/Member-Benefits/alpine-huts.php) and Tirol's [hut FAQs](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/hiking/refuge-huts):

- Broken-in walking boots with a grippy sole
- Waterproof jacket and trousers, a warm layer, hat and gloves, even in August
- Sun cream, sunglasses and a sun hat
- A hut sleeping bag (liner) and light hut slippers
- A head torch for the dormitory at night
- Small toiletries and a quick-drying towel
- First aid kit, a charged phone and a paper map or GPS
- Cash and your Alpine Club card if you have one
- Water bottle and snacks for the trail

## Trail grades, weather and safety

Austrian mountain paths are signposted with yellow signs, and the Austrian Alpine Club [grades them](https://www.alpenverein.at/portal/huetten-wege/wegenetz/wegekategorien/index.php) with a coloured dot. The grade reflects the hardest section before the destination named on the sign, assuming good weather and conditions:

- **Blue:** easier paths, which can still be narrow and steep.
- **Red:** mostly narrow and often steep, with possible sections where a fall would be serious and short secured passages.
- **Black:** narrow, often steep and exposed, with frequent secured sections or easy scrambling that needs your hands. Sure-footedness and a head for heights are essential.
- **Alpine routes** lead into open high-alpine terrain and are not mountain paths in this sense.

Tyrol has its own variation. Tirol's tourist board [describes](https://tyrol.com/travel-service/how-to-tirol/hiking-rules-of-behaviour) easy hiking trails there with a white circle on the yellow sign, alongside red and black mountain paths. Tirol's tourist board recommends black paths only for sure-footed, experienced walkers with a head for heights.

The same Tirol page summarises the Austrian Alpine Club's ten recommendations for safe hiking. Check the forecast carefully, since rain, wind and cold all raise the risk of accidents. Stay on marked paths, treat steep old snowfields with great caution, and tell someone your route and expected return. Falls from slipping or tripping are the most common cause of accidents, often when walkers are tired or rushing.

In an emergency, call **140**, the Alpine emergency number, or **112**, the European emergency number, or use the SOS EU ALP app, which can send your GPS position. Even on well-marked routes, the club's [Glockner Trail guide](https://www.alpenverein.at/portal_wAssets/docs/berg-aktiv/wege_touren/trekkingrouten/glocknertrail_english.pdf) warns that some sections can still be under old snow in July.

## Good first routes

Start with two or three nights on red-graded paths before attempting a long high route.

- **The Stubai Valley, near Innsbruck.** The [Stubai High Trail](https://www.stubai.at/en/stubai-high-trail/) links eight huts in a loop of nearly 80 km with more than 6,000 m of ascent, and the whole route takes eight days. The tourist board is clear that it demands sure-footedness, fitness and a head for heights. You can join or leave at any hut and reach the trailheads by public transport, so a stage or two makes a good taster.
- **The Karwendel.** Tirol's list of [weekend hut tours](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/hiking/hut-tours-for-a-weekend) includes the Classic Karwendel Crossing from Scharnitz, rated medium over three days, through Austria's largest nature park.
- **The Lechtal.** The valley's tourist board [lists](https://www.lechtal.at/en/activities/hiking/long-distance-hiking-trails-multi-day-tours.html) both the high, hut-to-hut Lechtaler Höhenweg, whose stages are mostly rated hard, and the gentler Lechweg, which appears on Tirol's list of [easy and moderately difficult long-distance trails](https://www.tyrol.com/activities/sport/hiking/long-distance-hiking-trails/easy-long-distance-hiking-trails).
- **The Hohe Tauern.** The [Glockner Trail](https://www.alpenverein.at/portal_wAssets/docs/berg-aktiv/wege_touren/trekkingrouten/glocknertrail_english.pdf) circles the Grossglockner in seven days, ending each day at a hut or inn. It needs no glacier crossings, but the Alpine Club describes it as a route for experienced alpine hikers and advises less experienced walkers to go with a qualified mountain guide.

## Before you go

- Check each hut's opening dates on the [Alpine Club list](https://www.alpenverein.at/portal/huetten-wege/huettenoeffnungszeiten.php) or the hut's own page, especially early and late in the season.
- Book beds through [Hut Reservation](https://www.hut-reservation.org/) or directly with the hut, and cancel promptly if your plans change.
- Read each stage's grade, length and height gain, and plan your days around the slowest walker.
- Carry a hut sleeping bag, slippers, a head torch and cash.
- Save 140 and 112 in your phone, and write your route in the hut book.

Hut walking rewards a slow pace: long days on the trail, dinner at a shared table and lights out at ten. Build up from a short circuit to a longer high route, and if you would rather walk a demanding route with a professional, you can look for certified mountain guides in the Tourground directory.
