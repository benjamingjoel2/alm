Early on a summer morning, an Alm is a quiet place. Dew sits on the grass, cowbells carry further than you expect, and the hut at the edge of the pasture has its benches out but its kitchen not yet open. Below, the valley is still in shade. This is the Austria many people picture, and it is real, but it is only one part of a country that runs from a broad, wine-growing river valley in the east to high glaciers in the west.

This guide is the one we would want before a first visit: how the country fits together, when to come, how to move around, and the small customs that make the days go smoothly.

## The shape of the country

The simplest way to picture Austria is as a long country that gets higher as you travel west. According to Austria's national tourist office, [the Alps cover almost two-thirds of Austria](https://www.austria.info/en-gb/inspiration/alps/), stretching from the Rätikon range in Vorarlberg in the west to the Gutenstein Alps in the east.

- **The Danube east.** Vienna sits on the Danube in the flatter north-east. Upstream, the river cuts through the Wachau, a [World Heritage cultural landscape of vineyard terraces, villages, monasteries and castles](https://www.austria.info/en-gb/highlights/unesco-world-heritage-sites/) running for 36 kilometres in Lower Austria, with Melk Abbey at its western end.
- **The lakes in the middle.** East of Salzburg, the Salzkammergut is lake and mountain country. Its core, Hallstatt–Dachstein, is on the same World Heritage list as a salt landscape shaped by mining since the Bronze Age.
- **The Alps west.** Tyrol and Vorarlberg are the high-mountain provinces, with Innsbruck as the one city where the peaks seem to start at the end of the street.
- **Carinthia south.** Across the main ridge of the Alps, Carinthia calls itself [the land of lakes in Austria's sunny south](https://www.visitcarinthia.at/activities/summer/lakes-experience/carinthia-the-land-of-lakes). Its tourist board describes Wörthersee as the largest lake in the province and one of the warmest in the Alps.

For a first trip, this east-to-west line matters more than provincial borders: most of the places people want to see sit on or near one main railway.

## When to go

Austria's tourist office describes the country as [a year-round destination](https://www.austria.info/en-us/planning/austria-at-a-glance/), and the season you choose changes the trip more than the route does.

- **Summer** is for hiking, cycling and lakes. In Carinthia, the tourist board says many lakes are pleasantly warm as early as May.
- **Winter** is for snow and skiing in the Alps, and for the Christmas markets in the cities (see our [Christmas markets guide](/stories/austria-christmas-markets/)).
- **Spring and autumn** are, in the tourist office's words, the seasons for cities, culture and food in milder weather.

Shoulder seasons have trade-offs. Some sights run reduced hours or guided-only visits in the colder months: [Melk Abbey](https://www.stiftmelk.at/en/visit-experience/opening-hours-prices-guided-tours/), for example, can be visited only on a guided tour for part of the winter. If one sight is the reason for your trip, check its own calendar before you fix dates.

## Getting around by train

Austria is one of the easiest countries in Europe to see without a car. The national operator is ÖBB, and its long-distance flagship is the Railjet, which ÖBB itself calls [its premium train](https://www.oebb.at/en/reiseplanung-services/im-zug/unsere-zuege).

The main line runs west from Vienna. ÖBB says trains run between [Vienna and Salzburg twice an hour on the Western Line](https://www.oebb.at/en/reiseplanung-services/oebb-zugverbindungen/zugverbindungen-oesterreich), and that Railjet Xpress services run hourly from Vienna via Linz, Salzburg and Innsbruck to Feldkirch. Carinthia is on a separate route south; ÖBB lists 26 daily services from Vienna to Klagenfurt.

A few things to know before you book:

- **The app does most of the work.** The [ÖBB app](https://www.oebb.at/en/tickets-kundenkarten/online-mobile-ticketing/oebb-app) sells tickets, shows platform and train number, and lets you pick a seat on a Railjet from a seat map.
- **Sparschiene is the cheaper fare.** ÖBB describes [Sparschiene as a limited, train-specific offer](https://www.oebb.at/en/tickets-kundenkarten/oesterreich-europa/sparschiene) that can be booked up to six months ahead. Each ticket is valid only on the train you chose, and the basic version cannot be cancelled or refunded. The Komfort version adds a seat reservation and some cancellation flexibility.
- **Seat reservations are optional but sensible.** ÖBB [advises reserving a seat](https://www.oebb.at/en/reiseplanung-services/vor-ihrer-reise/reservierung-sitzplatz) if you want to be sure of travelling on your first-choice train, and warns that on busy days demand can exceed capacity.
- **The KlimaTicket is a resident's tool.** The [KlimaTicket Ö](https://www.klimaticket.at/en/) covers scheduled public transport across Austria for a whole year. For a short visit, ordinary tickets are the norm.

If you do drive, Austria's motorways and expressways need a vignette for vehicles up to 3.5 tonnes. The tourist office's [vignette guide](https://www.austria.info/en-gb/planning/toll-vignette-and-go-box) explains that the motorway operator ASFINAG sells it online and through its app, and that from 1 December 2026 vignettes will be sold only in digital form. Digital 1-day and 10-day versions suit short trips. Some Alpine routes, such as the Brenner and Tauern motorways, carry a separate section toll on top.

## Entry rules in November 2026

Austria is in the Schengen area. For British visitors, the UK government says you can [travel without a visa for up to 90 days in any 180-day period](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/austria/entry-requirements), and that your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before you arrive and be valid for at least 3 months after the day you plan to leave. Other nationalities should check their own government's advice.

Two EU systems matter for non-EU visitors:

- **EES (Entry/Exit System).** The European Commission says EES has been [fully operational across all Schengen countries since 10 April 2026](https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/news/main-differences-between-ees-and-etias-what-travellers-need-know-2026-04-28_en). Short-stay visitors from outside the EU have their facial image and fingerprints recorded at the border, along with passport details and entry and exit dates. Allow extra time at the border on your first crossing.
- **ETIAS (travel authorisation).** In April 2026 the Commission said ETIAS was scheduled to start in the last quarter of 2026, with the exact date to be announced separately. It will apply to visa-free nationals and, once approved, be valid for three years or until the passport expires. We could not confirm a start date at the time of writing, so check the [official ETIAS page](https://travel-europe.europa.eu/en/etias) before you book and again before you travel.

## Money, Sundays and everyday customs

Austria uses the euro. Vienna's tourist board says you can [pay by card without problem](https://www.wien.info/en/travel-info/tourist-info/money-needs-352200) in most places, and that tipping is customary but scaled to how happy you were with the service. Its [visitor FAQ](https://www.wien.info/en/travel-info/frequently-asked-questions-346338) puts the usual tip at around 10% in restaurants and coffee houses, adds that there is no obligation, and notes that you can leave it in cash or add it when paying by card. If service is already included on the bill, do not pay it twice.

The same FAQ covers three things first-timers ask about:

- **Sunday closing.** Shops generally close on Sundays and holidays. In Vienna, Sunday shopping is limited to the large railway stations, the airport and museum shops. Buy picnic food and toiletries on Saturday.
- **Tap water.** Vienna's water is of very high quality and can be drunk straight from the tap. Bring a refillable bottle.
- **Language.** German is spoken, but the tourist board says no knowledge of it is needed to visit Vienna and that most residents speak English. A greeting and a thank-you in German are still appreciated everywhere.

### Coffee and wine

The Viennese coffee house is a good first lesson in Austrian pace. Coffee comes with a glass of water; the city's tourist board explains that this began as [somewhere to rest your spoon](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/coffeehouses/wiener-kaffeehaus-fun-facts-433242) and became a sign of good spring water. The Austrian UNESCO Commission lists [Viennese coffee house culture](https://www.unesco.at/en/culture/intangible-cultural-heritage/inventory/element/viennese-coffee-house-culture) as intangible cultural heritage, describing the coffee house as a place where time and space are consumed but only the coffee appears on the bill. Nobody will hurry you. Our [Vienna coffee houses guide](/stories/vienna-coffee-houses-guide/) has more.

Wine has its own ritual. A Viennese Heuriger, or wine tavern, serves its own wine, and the tourist board says a genuine one [can be recognised by a pine bush and a sign reading "Ausg'steckt"](https://www.wien.info/en/dine-drink/wine/viennese-heurige-347736), which also shows when it is open. Food is often a buffet of cold dishes, and some taverns are licensed to serve hot food too.

## Mountain safety basics

You do not need to be a mountaineer to enjoy the Alps, but even an easy walk from a cable-car station is in a different world from the valley. Austria's tourist office sets out [safety tips for mountain hiking](https://www.austria.info/en-us/inspiration/safety-tips-for-mountain-hiking/) that we would put in every first-timer's pocket:

- Check the route's length, elevation gain, difficulty and the weather forecast, and choose one that matches your fitness.
- Wear sturdy footwear with good grip and carry layers, a first-aid kit and a charged phone.
- Tell someone which route you are taking.
- If storms or fog threaten, or conditions deteriorate, turn back in good time.
- Give cows a wide berth, especially when calves are nearby, and keep dogs on a lead.

The Austrian government lists [140 for mountain rescue and 112 as the European emergency number](https://www.oesterreich.gv.at/en/themen/notfaelle_unfaelle_und_kriminalitaet/notrufnummern), both free from any phone. The tourist office notes that in Vorarlberg, mountain emergencies go to 144. For longer walks between huts, read our [hut-to-hut hiking guide](/stories/hut-to-hut-hiking-austrian-alps/).

## How to pace a first trip

The most common first-trip mistake is trying to see the whole country in a week. Austria rewards fewer bases and longer stays.

- **Two or three bases are plenty.** Vienna, Salzburg and Innsbruck sit along one railway, which makes them a natural first line through the country. Our [10-day train itinerary](/stories/austria-10-day-itinerary-by-train/) follows exactly that.
- **Use day trips for the countryside.** The Wachau and the Salzkammergut lakes work well from a city base. See our guides to the [Wachau by bike and boat](/stories/wachau-valley-by-bike-and-boat/) and [Hallstatt and the lakes](/stories/hallstatt-and-the-salzkammergut-lakes/).
- **Leave a spare day for weather.** Mountain views, lake boats and high cable cars depend on it. A free day lets you move the high-altitude plans to the clearest one.
- **Do not stack big sights.** One palace or museum in the morning and a walk or a coffee house in the afternoon is a very Austrian day.

## Before you go

- Check entry rules with your own government and the [EU's ETIAS page](https://travel-europe.europa.eu/en/etias), as they are changing in 2026.
- Download the ÖBB app and book long-distance trains early if you want Sparschiene fares.
- Reserve seats on Railjets for weekends and holiday periods.
- Shop on Saturday for anything you need on Sunday.
- Carry a refillable water bottle and a card that works abroad.
- For mountain days, pack proper footwear, layers and a charged phone, and save 140 and 112.
- If driving, buy a digital vignette before you reach the motorway.

Austria is easy to get right on a first visit, as long as you let it set the pace: a long breakfast, a train along the valley, a walk before the afternoon clouds build. If you would rather have someone shape the route with you, Danube & Alps' sample journeys are a useful place to start.
